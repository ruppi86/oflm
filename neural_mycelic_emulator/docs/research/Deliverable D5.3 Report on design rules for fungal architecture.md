1 Introduction

In this deliverable we report upon the definition of design rules for Fungal Architectures. These
rules target two key objectives that seek to offer a solution to the problem statement defined in
the project abstract:

As one of the primary consumers of environmental resource, the building industry
faces unprecedented challenges in needing to reduce the environmental impact of
current consumption practices. This applies to both the construction of the built
environment and resource consumption during its occupation and use. Where in-
cremental improvements to current practices can be realised, the net benefits are
often far outstripped by the burgeoning demands of rapidly increasing population
growth and urbanisation. Against the backdrop of this grand societal challenge, it
is necessary to explore approaches that envision a paradigm shift in how material is
sourced, processed and assembled to address the magnitude of these challenges in a
truly sustainable way, and which can even provide added value.

1. that the design rules embody an inclusive, equitability, affordable, sustainable construction
approach with low barriers to entry and built upon principles of ecological and social justice,
community practice and empowerment through accessible craft mastery.

2. that the rules lead to unique spatial, tectonic and architectural expressions - building upon
characteristics established by the first construction concept prototype (shown previously
at the start of section T5.1 - Growth Scaffolds).

We have approached the first objective by developing a construction concept that combines
two of humanity’s most enduring technologies - weaving and fermentation. The pan-cultural
Kagome weave technique is over 8000 years old. Weaves can be made from an abundant palette of
materials and the technique can be quickly learned. Combining this with solid-state-fermentation
techniques that can exploit ligno-cellulosic waste streams to create viable and performing material
composites, provides the basis for a novel and accessible approach to construction.

In approaching the second objective, particular attention has been paid to developing a digital
design workflow to allow open design exploration with specific focus on weave pattern topology,
morphology, structural performance and spatial organisation.

2 Focus areas of design development

The design rules and workflow presented in Section 3 - Design Rules, reflect significant revisions
to the original construction concept (Fig.1). These revisions have been informed by the many
experimental findings, empirical investigations of mycelium-based composite (MBC) mechanical
properties and 1:1 prototyping of assemblies (reported on in Deliverable D2.3) conducted across
the project period. Principal revisions to the original concept and objectives include:

1. the introduction of an independent Kagome gridshell acting as primary structural support

for an MBC enclosure

2. falsifying the notion of macro-scale morphological adaptations through mycelium growth

3. falsifying the notion of occupiable spaces being composed of monolithic living mycelium

4. needing to contain living mycelium in distinct and differentiated conditions to human oc-

cupants

5. the use of a single kagome woven surface for the application of MBC as opposed to a ‘filled

Kagome’ approach

6. the use of denatured MBC moulded panels, without Kagome weaves, to act as spatial
separators within the domain of the gridshell enabling the consideration of component
elements to spatial organisation

In this section, we briefly report upon the four core areas of investigation that have informed
the architectural development of the construction concept. These are, the Kagome modelling
approach, limits in mechanical perfomance of MBC, Kagome weaving with solid timber members
and broader design speculations that guide the development of the spatial vocabulary for Fungal
Architectures.

2.1 Kagome Modelling

In deliverable D5.1 - Growth Scaffolds, we reported upon the development of the Mesh Topo-
logy Adjustment Scheme (MTAS) - a method for designing principled Kagome weave patterns
using regular computational tri-meshes as a basis [1]. Our subsequent investigations in Kagome
modelling have developed a more generalised approach that overcomes a number of restrictions
of MTAS. These included:

1. manual locating of vertices for topology adjustment

2. sequences of singularity placement could result failed topology designs despite being phys-

ically weaveable

3. closed geometries failing due to circular dependencies in topology adjustment

4. a regular planar tri-mesh as a starting point required a strong design intuition to produce

complex weave pattern designs that approximate desired morphologies

Identification of the weavers and their interlacing is easily accomplished with the ’open’
polygon mesh. To accomplish this we exploit the ’right-hand-rule’ that maintains edge direction
for all mesh edges. We then elevate the start vertex of each edge along the vertex normal relative
to the mesh surface. This is elevated by the interlacing height and then a new line is drawn
between this elevated vertex and the unedited end vertex of the mesh edge. Performing a joining
of all coincident line vertices results in the definition of independent weavers and derives an
approximation of their interlacing. This saw-tooth geometry can be relaxed by generating a
b-spline curve through the vertices, and, in turn, used as a guide spline to model the material
dimensions (Fig.6).

2.2 Limits in mechanical performance of MBC

In deliverable D5.2 - Report on preliminary characterisation and modelling, we reported on a
material engineering review of the enzymatic activity of ligninolytic fungi [2]. Three principal
material design strategies for MBC systems were identified: densification (by dense packing,
cold or hot-pressing), composition (by introducing structuring elements, or modifying particles
and/or fibre properties), and supplementation (targeting mycelium properties based on chemical
tuning of the substrate). Of these three strategies, we conducted an experimental series targeting
composition, which remains the least investigated strategy in the MBC literature. Drawing upon
insights from the field of synthetic composite design, in which fibre composition is a primary
design vector for functional property tuning, we reported on a substrate composition approach
of orientated fibres in combination with different particle sizes and geometries for the bulk MBC
volume (Fig.12).

Figure 12: Compression series. (left) bulk volume substrate particle sizes. (right) four post-
compression samples illustrating different mechanical properties resulting from different struc-
turing approaches.

We demonstrated the significance of introducing structuring fibres using approaches such as
jacketing with hessian and internal reinforcement with reed or rattan fibres for both compressive
and flexural behaviour. With an optimal substrate bulk material particle size of 0.75 – 3.0 mm,
the introduction of common reed fibres coaxial to the load axis resulted in a 2.77-fold increase of
the compressive modulus [3]. The flexural modulus of a longitudinally rattan-reinforced group of
specimens was of 1.34 GPa for a density of 249.48 kg/m3 (control: 192.71 MPa, 232.24 kg/m3)
[4]. It is anticipated that these preliminary results can be improved upon with hybrid material
design strategies, for example, composition in combination with supplementation.

Despite these promising improvements in comparison to results reported on in the literature,
the mechanical performance of MBC remains magnitudes of scale below that normally required
for primary structure. Whilst we acknowledge below, in section 2.4, that design approaches exist
demonstrating the exploitation of compression-only structural form to accommodate the poor
mechanical properties of MBC in comparison to conventional materials used as primary structure
for construction, our design criteria led to the decision of separating out the structural role from
MBC materials and allocating this to a novel woven gridshell design.

2.4 Speculative design investigation for a Fungal Architecture

As reported in deliverable D2.3 section 3 (D2.3 - Structural soundness is verified), the literature
provides evidence of design methods for utilising MBC in load-bearing roles for structurally
informed spatial configurations. However, a key limitation is that structural form must be
constrained to compression only designs [5, 6, 7], restricting the space of possible morphologies
that can be expressed.

For the FUNGAR project, this represents a fundamental restriction in the possible deploy-
ment of computationally active materials. A key finding of the FUNGAR project, by partner
UWE, has been that living mycelium-based materials exhibit different spiking characteristics
based on stimulation with light and mechanical loading respectively [8]. The consideration of
this finding in the context of architectural application, suggests that placement of living mycelium
in specifically designed locations could lead to qualitatively differentiated patterns of activity res-
ulting from a given morphology in interaction with its environment. This aligns conceptually
with theoretical understandings developed within the field of Morphological Computation [9, 10,
11, 12] in which morphology is understood as computational resource which can be designed with
the aim of ‘out-sourcing’ computational control requirements. Therefore, we have developed our
construction logic to permit the largest possible morphological design space, with the assumption
being that responsive programming for a living architecture can be partially approached through
the consideration of a design form within a given geographical and embodied context.

Initial explorations to were conducted to search for morphologies that could give rise to specific
information generation characteristics, given a specific geographical location and assuming sunlit
conditions. In figure 16 a simulation is conducted to search for positions to place living mycelium
that can be triggered through light stimuli to produce a signal. A number of points are sought to
embed a notional ’majority-rule’ logic. In figure 17 the positioning search space is restricted to
a particular spatial region. These initial studies provided a conceptual basis for considering how
living mycelium, with it’s sensing capabilities, could be used as a design driver and impact macro
and meso-scale geometries for a Fungal Architecture. In combination with the Kagome weaving,
which provides a rationalised and materially efficient approach to realising approximations to any
complex manifold morphology, we have the basis for developing a unique architectural expression
and construction concept that embeds novel functionalities based on novel materials.

From this basis, we conducted an iterative design exploration to refine the construction
concept and its expression through use-inspired scenarios. As a first step, we explored how
the conceptual separation between load-bearing structure and enclosing skin could be made spa-
tially manifest. We accomplish this by considering the roles of structure and enclosure being
served by two different Kagome weaves (Fig.18). By interlacing these weaves, we create the
opportunity for a rich and varied topography to be established. Selective colonisation of weave
for enclosure allows for light and view to be programmed into the surface (Fig.??).

Figure 19 shows a detailed study of the relationship between the primary gridshell Kagome
and surface enclosure performed by a mycelium composite layer sandwiched between two Kagome
weaves.

A notional site was chosen to exercise these design rules in the design of a hypothetical
architectural proposition. The proposition develops a strong landscape quality with an undu-
lating surface interacting with the ground condition and creating a variety of elevated, sunken
or surrounded conditions. The spatial organisation is guided by principles of asymmetric and
wandering routes leading to contained spaces. Internal spatial divisions are determined through
single curved walls that exist independently from the Kagome gridshell structure. This provides
planning flexilibility and differentiation from the geometry of the structure above.

2.5 Conclusions

In this section we have reported on key aspects of design investigation that have developed and
revised our original construction concept. Our revised Kagome modelling method provides a
generalised approach for generating principled Kagome weave patterns to achieve any morphology
that can be modelled as a manifold polygonal mesh. We have determined that MBC composite
mechancial performance is not adequate for use as primary structural material in our case. We
have demonstrated a novel Kagome weaving approach to realising timber gridshells using solid
timber weavers, that preserves the principles of Kagome production. Finally, we have integrated
these findings to develop novel spatial design vocabulary for Fungal Architectures.

This work establishes the basis for the design rules which have been encoded into a digital

design workflow described in the following section.

3 Design rules

3.1 Workflow

In this section we describe the integration of design rules into a computational workflow for
designing Fungal Architectures. The workflow is built around the consideration of four areas of
design influence:

1. structural analysis

2. spatial division

3. environmental analysis

4. indentifying sites for living mycelium acting as sensors and signal generators

In the following subsections, we briefly describe their operation and dependencies.

3.1.1. Structurally informed design

A spatial envelope is created by moving the control points of a flat surface vertically, resulting in
a series of random and structurally inefficient forms. A maximum cantilever distance and angle
is prescribed. The most efficient point outside the supported region is identified (by proximity to
the floor and to other surrounding features) and moved to the floor, creating a structural column
that can incrementally increase in radius as the surrounding control points descend until the span
is within the maximum bounds. If descent exceeds a maximum iteration count, the strategy is
disregarded and new points are selected.
It
also considers a constraint for minimum heights for habitation. Once the surface is defined, a
primary triangulation occurs and is relaxed to achieve a morphology achievable with Kagome.
This is then tested structurally analysed to determine viability. Modifications are made to the
control points and the process repeats. Once a structurally viable form has been established,
the secondary weave is generated through an interference pattern which creates a surface that
intersects the primary structure, with a minimum frequency and maximum amplitude ensuring
the distance directly between support points, and geodesically across the secondary weave, do
not exceed a predetermined maximum. When the secondary weave is derived from the primary
mesh it is possible to ensure the weavers do not intersect and so the two layers are topologically
independent but sparsely interwoven. Figure 22 illustrates the steps and potential feedbacks for
this component of the design workflow.

If this fails repeatedly the iteration is ignored.

3.1.3. Environmental analysis

Given a particular geographical location, the predominant prevailing wind directions are can
be constructed from historical data which can be further refined to include the influence of the
immediate surroundings. These are then imposed on the form which responds with deformation
to amplify the negative pressure created on the leeward face. This could increasing the draw
of strategically placed vents and passively cool the interior environment. These ridges are also
useful in the external collection and storage of rain water, making a suitable environment for
further colonisation of the external surface by other flora. In the future, this could also be a
possible trigger for a behavioural response of the building, or extending the analysis to other
environmental variables. The current structural generation guides the designer towards viable
morphologies and excludes those that are not viable. It is however a holistic process for generating
a primary and secondary weave that responds to environmental stimuli and constraints, and is an
apt demonstrator of the proposed system. In the following section, we extend the environmental
analysis to help inform the placement of placement of components, specifically living mycelium
for active sensing and signalling.

3.2.1. System specification

To measure the mycelium activity, a high-resolution data logger equipped with a 20-bit A/D
converter and custom scripts is used, along with a pair of stainless steel needle electrodes. The
electrodes are inserted approximately 15 mm to 2 cm into the substrate. The activity is sampled
at a rate of one measurement per second, and the average value is recorded. The measured
values obtained from Picolog are saved in a CSV file format on the Raspberry Pi. Typically,
the sampling frequency is set to 500 Hz, and the obtained values are mapped to an RGB color
gradient (see Fig.31).

The lighting device utilises the Digital Multiplex (DMX) protocol, which is a unidirectional
lighting control protocol enabling sophisticated lighting sequences to be controlled from a single
source. The control box (Supersweet, Blinkinlabs), is equipped with a built-in WiFi connection
and provides ten outputs, each referred to as a universe. Each universe can accommodate up to
512 channels, with three channels dedicated to controlling the RGB LED strip.

3.2.2. Mapping of sense data to output

Regarding the mapping of sensory data to output, simply mapping the activity value within
a fixed range may result in low contrast lighting, where the colour representation only shows
spikes or remains the same throughout (Fig.32). To address this, we employed a data windowing
technique, or ’bucketing’, to record values over a period of 1 to 3 minutes. This allows us to
determine the average, highest and lowest values, which are then used to adjust the color gradient
(Fig.33). The lighting animation typically operates at a frame rate of 30 to 50 frames per second.
The signal values received from Picolog are stored in an animation CSV file, resulting in a delay of
approximately three minutes in streaming the activity to the display, due to the signal collection
required for the animation.

The display presents the activity from the past three minutes, and the frame rate of the anim-
ation is controlled based on the current average values, whether they are decreasing or increasing.
The rhythm rate ranges from 23 to 40 frames per second. The web interface integrates both the
electrical signal information from the mycelium and environmental data. The background of
the interface visually represents the current activity, while the palette in the bottom-left corner
provides the user with various color options. The past activity records are stored in the lower
left section, allowing users to click and playback previous activity.

4 Conclusions and perspectives

In this deliverable, we have reported upon key design investigations leading to the definition
of design rules as manifested in a digital workflow, to enable the proposition of Fungal Archi-
tectures. We have developed a novel construction concept based on the combination of two of
humanities oldest, and most enduring, technologies - fermentation and weaving. The construc-
tion concept has been nuanced and revised in light of various experimental results and findings
conducted throughout the project. This has led to a strong association between the possibilities
of Kagome weaving to approximate arbitrary design geometries and the sensing capabilities of
living mycelium. We have hypothesised that macro morphology of a Fungal Architecture could
be considered as the basis for a computationally performing architecture, specific to location.
We identify this as a promising area for future research.

We have also shown that the construction concept can be used to achieve such computational
goals, mediated through shape and in dialogue with many other architectural design factors, in-
cluding spatial organisation, structural performance and material specification. The design rules
developed for Fungal Architectures extend beyond a material focus and establish a framework for
generating novel architectural designs that embody unique spatial and tectonic qualities. Futher-
more, the design rules embody a construction approach that is grounded in fundamental and
accessible technologies with low barriers to entry. This not only extends these technologies into
novel domains of application, but also underscores an effort to make an accessible and inclusive
architecture.

We have also reported upon investigations for interfacing living mycelium in ways to have
direct and real-time architectural consequence, through a lighting system directly linked to liv-
ing mycelium. This is the first time, as far as we are aware, that a living mycelium interface
has been linked to provide direct internal modification of ambient lighting within a use-inspired
architectural context.

In conclusion, we see a promising future architectural (but necessarily and essentially cross-
disciplinary) research area emerging in which the deeper integration of living mycelium - or
other living materials - act as fully integrated building management systems. A key opportunity
here would be consider biological linkages growing to expand beyond the boundary of individual
buildings and spaces, to create an inherently linked network at ecological scale and with ecological
imperatives.

Further research should also focus on engaging with communities to test transfers of know-
ledge in the making of weaves and MBC materials and the know-how for the exploitation of local
resources to create vernacular architectural expressions that are ecologically localised.
